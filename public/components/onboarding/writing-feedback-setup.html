Welcome to Writing Feedback

Draft formative feedback on student writing, with course staff approving every word that reaches a student

Feedback that quotes the student's own writing

Writing Feedback reads a verified student submission against a rubric you approved, and drafts formative comments anchored to exact passages in that submission. It does not rewrite the student's sentences and it does not produce a model answer.

Instructors and teaching assistants have the same access here. Everything this tutorial describes — assignments, rubrics, review, approval, and release — is open to all course staff.

How writing gets in:

  • Create an assignment directly, giving it a title, instructions, and an optional due date.
  • Or import one from Canvas, which brings across the assignment description and, when the Canvas assignment has one, the rubric you authored there.
  • Add submissions as pasted text or uploaded documents.
  • Verify the text before anything is generated — course staff confirm the extracted text matches what the student actually wrote.

Assignments and submissions can come from Canvas or be entered manually.

Verification is not optional. Feedback is generated only from text course staff have verified, because every quotation in the feedback must exist exactly in the student's submission.

This tutorial is a simulation. These controls do not import, create, or save anything.

Assignment Details and the Rubric

The rubric page has two numbered parts, and feedback waits until you approve what they produce

1 · Assignment details

Before the grid, the rubric records what the assignment actually is: the task, the audience, the purpose, the constraints students must work within, the learning outcomes it assesses, and your grading intent. That context is what keeps drafted feedback aimed at your assignment rather than at generic "good writing".

2 · Rubric

A rubric is one grid. Criteria are its rows, performance levels are its shared columns, and each cell holds a point award and a descriptor saying what that level looks like for that criterion.

Criteria — the rows

What the feedback is asked to judge. A new assignment starts with these three, and you can rename, reword, reweight, add, or remove them.

  • Organization — how effectively the text is staged and held together for this task
  • Content — how accurately and completely the text represents the subject of the assignment
  • Interpersonal Positioning — how effectively the writer positions the reader for the stated audience and purpose

Performance levels — the columns

Between 2 and 8, ranked contiguously from 1, shared by every row.

  1. Weak
  2. Developing
  3. Proficient
  4. Exemplary

Where those three criteria come from. They are the three kinds of meaning any piece of writing makes at once: how it is organized, what it says, and how it positions its reader. Keeping them separate is what lets a comment explain why a passage lands the way it does instead of only labelling it good or weak. An imported Canvas rubric replaces them outright.

The Fill again from instructions button drafts descriptor wording for the cells from your assignment instructions. It never changes your points and never removes a row — the assistant contributes wording, not decisions about what is assessed.

Saving a draft is not approving it. The two are deliberately separate actions. Feedback generation stays blocked until an approved rubric exists.

You can keep changing the rubric after approval. Add or remove criteria and levels whenever you need to; each structural change simply produces a new rubric version. Feedback generated earlier keeps rendering against the version that produced it, so nothing you have already reviewed is reinterpreted behind you.

Lab Reports Carry a Second Rubric

Writing and technical quality are judged separately, because they are different questions

Mark an assignment as a lab report and it carries two rubrics instead of one. The Writing rubric judges how the report is written. The Technical rubric judges the engineering: whether the method is sound, whether the data supports the claims, whether the analysis and its limitations hold up.

You edit both on the same rubric page and approve them the same way. The assignment details you entered are shared, so you describe the task once.

Writing rubric

Organization, content, and how the report positions its reader.

  • Structure and staging of the report
  • Accuracy and sufficiency of what is explained
  • Stance, hedging, and technicality for the stated audience

Technical rubric

The department's evaluation form for engineering content.

  • Method and experimental reasoning
  • Data, results, and their interpretation
  • Stated assumptions and limitations

The technical rubric judges whether the argument holds together, not whether it agrees with theory. It will not tell a student a result is wrong for deviating from an expected value, supply the correct value, or redo the experiment.

It reads text and tables only. It cannot see a figure, a graph, or an image. When a judgement would depend on one, it says so and declines to make it, recording what it could not assess in the internal review flags only course staff see. It does not guess at a figure it cannot read.

The technical results are read-only during review. They appear alongside the writing feedback as Feedback by technical rubric criterion and Priority technical revision goals. To change what the technical rubric assesses, edit and re-approve it on the rubric page, then generate again.

Reviewing Generated Feedback

Every comment points at an exact passage you can check

Generation is queued rather than instant — the page tells you it is running and refreshes when the draft is ready. What arrives is a set of anchored comments. Each one names the criterion it addresses and quotes the passage it is about, so you can verify the judgement rather than take it on trust.

Organization Developing
"The results are shown in Figure 2. The heat transfer coefficient dropped. We also measured pressure drop."

Three findings are presented in sequence without stating how they relate. Consider signalling which observation is the main result and which support it, so the reader knows what the section is arguing.

Interpersonal Positioning Proficient
"Fouling resistance typically increases asymptotically over the first year of service (Kern, 1965)."

"Typically" marks this as a general tendency rather than a certainty, and the attribution shows the reader where the claim comes from. This is the stance to repeat in the discussion section, where several claims are currently stated flatly and without a source.

What else the review page gives you

  • A linguistic account, not just a verdict. Comments are grouped by what the language is doing — its organizational, content, and interpersonal function — so a student learns why a passage lands the way it does.
  • Glossary terms. A term used in feedback can point at your course's own definition, so students meet one vocabulary rather than several.
  • Course material pointers. Where a finding matches something you have already taught, the feedback can name that material. Student writing is never sent to the material search to find it.
  • Suggested grading. Each criterion reports the level the assistant chose and why, with a total. It is a reading aid for you only.

Quotations are verified, not paraphrased. A comment can only quote text that appears exactly in the verified submission. If the assistant cannot ground a judgement in the student's own words, it does not make it.

Suggested grading never leaves this page. It is derived when you ask to see it, is never stored, and never reaches the student PDF, a release payload, or Canvas. No grade is written back to Canvas by this feature.

Edit anything. Rewrite a comment, delete one you disagree with, or add your own. Your revisions are saved to the audit history, and your version is what the student sees.

Approving and Releasing

Two separate decisions, both yours

Approving marks the feedback as finished and ready. Releasing is what actually delivers it. Keeping them apart means you can finish reviewing a stack of submissions and still choose when students receive them.

This example submission has been reviewed and its comments edited. Approve it to continue.

Approved

The feedback is now marked approved. In your real course the release card appears next, showing exactly what the student would receive, with a preview before anything is sent. You can also download what the student gets: a summary PDF, their text with the comments highlighted on it, or both together.

  • Students never see confidence scores, internal review flags, model details, or staff-only notes.
  • Release is blocked while a rubric has changed since generation, so a student cannot receive feedback judged against a rubric that no longer applies.
  • Releasing a numeric grade stays blocked until the approved rubric carries a complete points mapping — a partial one would let a number be invented.
  • Nothing is delivered automatically. Release is always an action you take.
