Assignment Details and the Rubric

Three numbered steps, and feedback waits until you approve the rubric they produce

1 · Assignment details

The task, audience, purpose, constraints, learning outcomes, and grading intent — the context that aims feedback at your assignment rather than generic "good writing".

2 · Rubric

One grid: criteria are rows, performance levels are shared columns, and each cell holds a point band and a descriptor.

Criteria — the rows

Starting points only: rename, reweight, add, or remove them.

  • Organization — how the text is staged
  • Content — how accurately it covers the subject
  • Interpersonal Positioning — how it positions its reader

Performance levels — the columns

Between 2 and 8, ranked contiguously from 1, shared by every row.

  1. Weak
  2. Developing
  3. Proficient
  4. Exemplary

Saving a draft is not approving it. Generation stays blocked until an approved rubric exists. Help covers where these criteria come from, Fill again from instructions, and rubric versioning.

Lab Reports Carry a Second Rubric

Writing and technical quality are judged separately, because they are different questions

Answer Lab report assignment when you create or import one, and it carries two rubrics. Both are edited and approved on the same page and share one set of assignment details.

Writing rubric

How the report is written.

  • Structure and staging of the report
  • Accuracy and sufficiency of what is explained
  • Stance, hedging, and technicality

Technical rubric

The department's evaluation form for engineering content.

  • Method and experimental reasoning
  • Data, results, and their interpretation
  • Stated assumptions and limitations

It judges whether the argument holds together, not whether it agrees with theory. It reads text and tables only: where a judgement would need a figure, it declines and says so in the staff-only review flags.

Reviewing Generated Feedback

Every comment points at an exact passage you can check

Generation is queued; the page refreshes when the draft is ready. Review runs in two steps. Annotations comes first: each comment names its criterion and quotes its passage, so you can check the judgement rather than trust it. Next opens Summary, redrafted from your annotations if you changed any. Lab reports keep Technical and Writing tabs on both steps, and your final annotations are the ones printed for the student.

Organization Developing
"The results are shown in Figure 2. The heat transfer coefficient dropped. We also measured pressure drop."

Three findings in sequence, with nothing stating how they relate. Signal which observation is the main result and which support it, so the reader knows what the section argues.

Interpersonal Positioning Proficient
"Fouling resistance typically increases asymptotically over the first year of service (Kern, 1965)."

"Typically" marks a tendency rather than a certainty, and the attribution shows where the claim comes from. Repeat this stance in the discussion, where several claims are stated flatly.

What else the review page gives you

  • A linguistic account, not just a verdict: comments grouped by what the language does.
  • Glossary terms pointing at your course's own definitions.
  • Course material pointers, found without sending student writing to the material search.
  • Suggested grading, a staff-only reading aid that never reaches a student.
  • Full editing: your version is what the student sees.

Quotations are verified, not paraphrased. A judgement that cannot be grounded in the student's own words is not made at all.

Approving and Releasing

Two separate decisions, both yours

Approving marks the feedback finished. Releasing delivers it. Apart, they let you review a stack of submissions and still choose when students receive them.

This example has been reviewed and edited. Approve it to continue.

Approved

In your course the release card appears next with one Release to Canvas action, stating what the student will receive. It sends the student-facing PDF comment and, if you saved a staff-final assessment, the grade.

  • Students never see confidence scores, internal review flags, model details, or staff notes.
  • Release is blocked while a rubric has changed since generation.
  • Feedback is released once per submission; a correction means a new attempt.
  • Nothing is delivered automatically. Release is always an action you take.

Writing Feedback drafts formative comments on student writing, anchored to exact passages and judged against a rubric course staff approved.

Who this is for: instructors and teaching assistants have identical access to this workspace.

Getting writing in: create an assignment directly or import one from Canvas, then add submissions as text or documents.

Verification: course staff confirm the extracted text matches the student's actual writing before any feedback is generated. This is what makes exact quotation possible.

Importing from Canvas brings across the assignment description and, where the Canvas assignment has one, the rubric you authored there. A manual assignment takes a title, instructions, and an optional due date. Submissions arrive as pasted text or uploaded documents.

Assignment details and the rubric

The rubric page has three steps. 1 · Describe the assignment holds the task, audience, purpose, constraints, learning outcomes, and grading intent. 2 · Build the marking grid is the rubric itself. 3 · Approve it separates saving a draft from approving the rubric for generation.

The grid: criteria are rows, performance levels are shared columns, and each cell holds a point band and a descriptor. Limits are 1 to 10 criteria and 2 to 8 levels, ranked contiguously from 1.

The starting criteria are Organization, Content, and Interpersonal Positioning — the three kinds of meaning writing makes at once. They are a starting point, not a fixture: rename, reweight, add, or remove them, and an imported Canvas rubric replaces them entirely.

Points: give a criterion its weight and Space points evenly divides it across the levels. A weight too small to separate every level makes neighbouring columns share a value, and the grid warns you.

The Fill again from instructions button proposes descriptor wording from the assignment instructions. It does not change your weights or remove rows.

Approval: saving a draft and approving it are separate actions, and generation stays blocked until an approved rubric exists. You can keep changing the rubric afterwards — each structural change makes a new version, and earlier feedback stays tied to the version that produced it.

Lab reports

An assignment created as a Lab report assignment carries two rubrics: a Writing rubric for how the report is written, and a Technical rubric for the engineering content. Both are edited and approved on the rubric page, and they share the assignment details.

Consistency, not agreement. The technical rubric judges whether the report's own argument holds together. It does not mark a result wrong for deviating from theory, supply correct values, or recompute the experiment.

Text and tables only. It cannot see figures, graphs, or images. Where a judgement would need one, it declines and records what it could not assess in the internal review flags, rather than guessing.

Review happens in two steps. First go through the annotations, switching between Technical and Writing. Then press Next: if you changed any annotations, the summary and suggested grades are redrafted from them, and every summary section can be edited before you save and approve.

Technical results are read-only during review. Technical annotations appear first, with Feedback by technical rubric criterion and Priority technical revision goals beneath them. To change what the technical rubric assesses, edit and re-approve it on the rubric page, then generate again.

Reviewing feedback

Generation is queued; the page refreshes when the draft is ready.

Each comment names its criterion and quotes the exact passage it addresses. Quotations must appear verbatim in the verified submission — the assistant will not make a judgement it cannot ground in the student's own words.

Comments are grouped by what the language is doing, and can point at your course glossary and your course materials. Student writing is never sent to the material search.

Suggested grading is a reading aid for course staff. A grade is released only from the staff-final assessment you save and approve.

You can rewrite, delete, or add comments. Your revisions are saved to the audit history and your edited version is what the student receives.

Approval and release

Approving marks feedback as finished. Releasing delivers it. They are separate so you can control timing.

Press Approve to continue. Nothing is saved — this is a fixed example.

Student-facing output never includes confidence scores, internal review flags, model metadata, or staff-only notes. Numeric release stays blocked until the approved rubric carries a complete points mapping — a partial one would let a number be invented.

One release per submission. The release card offers a single Release to Canvas action with no separate preview step, and a correction after release means a new attempt rather than a second release. Release is also blocked while a rubric has changed since generation, so nobody receives feedback judged against a rubric that no longer applies.
